## Element of Biotechnology & Bioethics….Recap

### Biotechnology Defined

"Any technological application that uses biological systems, living organisms or derivatives thereof, to make or modify products or processes for specific use."

- Biotechnology can be traditional or modern.
- Both can create ethical issues.
- Ethical concerns are generally greater with modern biotechnology because it allows more direct manipulation of living organisms and their genetic material.

## Bioethics

- **Bioethics** = study of ethical issues associated with life.
- It includes:
  - Medical ethics
  - Environmental ethics
  - Ethical issues created by biotechnology
- Bioethics has developed alongside biotechnology.
- It goes beyond traditional medical ethics.
- Bioethical issues may occur at:
  - Personal level
  - Local level
  - National level
  - Regional level
  - Global level

## What should guide ethical decision making? Irrespective of scale…decisions will be made

### Autonomy

- Respect a person's right and ability to make their own choices.
- People have different values and attitudes towards biotechnology.
- Every person should be respected as an equal individual with their own values.
- Their right to make choices must be considered in discussions and decisions.

## ….. guide to ethical decision making..

### Rights

- Bioethical rights can be difficult because some human rights may not be legally recognised in every society.
- **Ethics ≠ law.**
- Something can be ethically required even when the law does not require it.
- Ethics can therefore demand a higher standard of behaviour than the law.

## ….. guide to ethical decision making..

### Beneficence

- **Beneficence = doing good / acting in someone's best interest.**
- Society generally tries to improve people's quality of life.
- We should try to do good rather than simply avoid doing harm.
- Failure to help when we can help may be considered an ethical error.
- Beneficence supports research that improves:
  - Health
  - Agriculture
  - Environment
- Experimentation can be ethically supported when it is intended to produce benefits.
- People have an obligation to help others pursue legitimate interests.
- Life-saving technology should ideally be shared with people who need it, even if they cannot afford it.

## ….. guide to ethical decision making..

### Do no harm

- When benefits and risks conflict, a balance must be achieved.
- Even when the intention is good, unnecessary harm should be avoided.
- This principle supports:
  - Respect for human life
  - Human integrity
  - Justice
  - Confidentiality
  - Philanthropy

## ….. guide to ethical decision making..

### Justice

- Society contains many individuals whose lives and interests deserve equal respect.
- One person's freedom cannot completely override the rights of others.
- Individual autonomy therefore has limits.
- People's well-being, values and choices should be respected fairly.
- We must also consider future generations.
- Today's decisions should not unfairly harm people who will live in the future.
- Internationally, questions arise about who should own and benefit from shared genetic resources.

## ….. guide to ethical decision making..

### Confidentiality

- Personal information should normally remain private.
- Exceptions may apply when:
  - Criminal activity is involved.
  - Another person is directly at risk of avoidable harm.
- Genetic information is particularly sensitive.
- Privacy of genetic information should therefore be strongly protected.

## ….. guide to ethical decision making..

### Animal Rights

Important questions:

- How should humans use animals while pursuing benefits through biotechnology?
- What moral status should animals have?

Factors that can influence the moral status of animals include:

- Ability to think.
- Ability to recognise/be aware of family members.
- Ability to feel pain.
- Being alive.

Main idea:

- Inflicting pain is bad.
- If animals are used, unnecessary pain should be avoided.

## ….. guide to ethical decision making

### Environmental Ethics.

- Humans depend on a healthy environment for survival.
- Protecting the environment can be justified because human life depends on it.
- Biodiversity should be protected.
- Ecosystems are delicately balanced.
- Introducing new organisms may disturb this balance.
- A major concern of modern biotechnology is the possible environmental effect of introducing new organisms.
- Future generations should inherit a habitable environment that has not been permanently damaged.

## Taking Bioethics Decisions

- Good biotechnology decisions require balancing conflicting ethical principles.
- Biotechnology brings both:
  - Risks
  - Benefits
- Ethical decision-making must balance these risks and benefits.
- **Utilitarianism** tries to produce the greatest happiness/benefit.
- It can be difficult to compare different amounts of happiness and harm.
- Decisions should consider the values of all affected people.
- Ethics should not simply be treated as identical to morality.
- When achieving the greatest good is difficult, we should at least try to achieve the least harm.

## Taking Bioethics Decisions….. Some guides

### Having the facts

Ask:

- Where did the information come from?
- Is it correct?
- Is it recent?
- Is the source reliable?
- How should contradictory facts be handled?

### Managing Values and Beliefs

- Everyone has beliefs and values.
- They give meaning to people's lives.
- Values are often influenced by culture.
- Different values and beliefs should be respected.

### Rational Principles

Check whether decisions are consistent with:

- Non-Maleficence
- Individual autonomy
- Beneficence
- Justice

### Extrinsic factors

- Factors related to the consequences or effects of biotechnology.

### Intrinsic factors

- Factors related to whether biotechnology is considered right or wrong **in itself**.

## Ten Step process model for ethical decision making

1. Stop, think and identify the situation or problem.
2. Construct a description.
3. Whose problem is it?
4. Review in terms of the ethical framework.
5. Consider legal, moral principles and values.
6. Identify the support that is available.
7. Identify courses of action.
8. Evaluate the outcome, using supervision where appropriate.
9. Regularly check the personal impact of these events.

## General Background / recap..

- Humans have used biotechnology for centuries.
- Traditional biotechnology helped improve food production, processing and medicine.
- Traditional biotechnology did not always create major ethical fears.
- Modern biotechnology has changed traditional methods and created new concerns about:
  - Ethics
  - Health
  - Safety
  - Environment
- Powerful modern techniques can create new risks and ethical complications.
- These risks must be properly understood and managed.

## Biotech ethics; Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic arguments

Ethical concerns about biotechnology broadly fall into two groups:

### Intrinsic arguments

- Biotechnology is considered wrong **in itself**.
- If something is intrinsically wrong, its good consequences or good intentions cannot make it morally right.

### Extrinsic arguments

- Biotechnology is considered wrong because of its **consequences**.
- Focuses on possible:
  - Risks
  - Harm
  - Social effects
  - Economic effects

## Crop Biotech- Intrinsic Concerns

Intrinsic concerns ask whether crop biotechnology is:

- Blasphemous
- Unnatural
- Disrespectful

If crop biotechnology is intrinsically wrong, its consequences or intentions do not change that basic judgement.

## Intrinsic Concern… Is crop biotech Blasphemous?

Two contrasting positions:

1. Manipulating plant genes may be considered **"playing God."**
2. Humans may instead be seen as participating in creation by modifying plants, similar to the changes humans have made through agriculture and breeding for thousands of years.

## …..is it blasphemous?

Some religious arguments against modern biotechnology are based on:

1. God created a perfect natural order.
2. Manipulating DNA and crossing species boundaries interferes with that order.
3. Such interference may be seen as trying to displace or challenge the Creator.

## …is it blasphemous? All religions do not agree!

### Hindus

- The supreme being continually creates and withdraws worlds.
- Creation is seen as an ongoing cycle.
- Humans have an important responsibility as custodians of creation.

### Judaism

- Humans have strong responsibilities towards nature.
- The earth belongs to the Lord.
- Humans do not have unlimited/unconditional rights over land.

## …is it blasphemous? All religions do not agree!

### Buddhism

- Humans should have a responsible relationship with nature.
- Taking animal life is prohibited.
- Humans have responsibility towards creation.
- Humans can achieve enlightenment but are not necessarily superior to all other forms of life.

### Christianity

- Christianity has no single unanimous position against modern biotechnology.
- Some interpretations say humans have a privileged position/dominion over nature.
- Some theologians see biotechnology as humans working with God as co-creators.
- Different Christian groups have different views.

## …is it blasphemous? Synthesis

### Species Boundary

- Crop biotechnology can transfer genes from one species to another.
- However, not all religions consider species boundaries sacred or permanently fixed.

### Agricultural

- Ethical concerns may be stronger for animal biotechnology than plant biotechnology.
- Main reason: animals are considered sentient/able to feel, while plants generally are not.

### Most concerns

- Most ethical concerns regarding modern crop biotechnology are connected with:
  - Environment
  - Biodiversity

### Traditional

- Traditional breeding also changes/interferes with nature.
- If interfering with the created order is blasphemous, traditional breeding would have the same problem.
- Such human interference has existed for thousands of years.
- Therefore, it is difficult to build a universal argument against modern biotechnology based only on blasphemy.

## Intrinsic Concern… Is crop biotech unnatural?

Two contrasting positions:

1. Changing what is natural may disturb harmony within living organisms and between organisms and their environment.
2. Movement of organisms and genes occurs naturally too; biotechnology may simply increase the speed and scale of such changes.

The question becomes:

- Is biotechnology actually unnatural?

## ……is crop biotech unnatural?

Arguments for considering biotechnology unnatural:

1. Nature is valuable and good in itself.
2. Biotechnology interferes with nature, especially natural species boundaries.
3. Therefore, modern biotechnology is intrinsically wrong.

Two important questions:

- What is natural and what is unnatural?
- Is being natural necessarily good?

## What is natural? What is unnatural?

Depending on context, "natural" may mean:

- Usual
- Normal
- Right
- Appropriate
- Uncultivated
- Innate
- Spontaneous

Questions arise:

- Are unnatural things simply the opposite of these?
- What about table eggs?
- What about broiler chickens?
- What about other products created through human intervention?

Important points:

- Modern civilisation depends heavily on human interference with nature.
- Humans have accepted many things that were once "unnatural" as normal.
- This has existed since humans domesticated plants and animals.
- Crossing species boundaries has existed for thousands of years and can also occur naturally.
- Biotechnology mainly makes these changes:
  - Faster
  - More obvious
- Therefore, the natural/unnatural argument can be difficult to define clearly.

## Is being natural necessarily good?

- Something being natural does not automatically mean it is good.
- Nature contains:
  - Natural toxins
  - Harmful substances
- Many useful medicines and vaccines are not naturally occurring.
- This raises the question:
  - Why accept biotech medicines but reject biotech foods simply because they are "unnatural"?
- Nature itself can produce harmful and cruel outcomes.
- Therefore, ethical right and wrong cannot be decided only by asking whether something is natural.
- The fact that something occurs naturally does not automatically mean humans are morally forbidden from interfering.
- Even if natural species barriers exist, their existence alone does not tell us what humans **ought** to do.

## Intrinsic Concern ….Is crop biotech disrespectful?

Two contrasting positions:

1. Genetic transfer between species, controlling nature and placing this control in private hands through patents may be considered disrespectful.
2. Genetic engineering is extremely complex and impressive and may create a sense of wonder and reverence towards life.

Question:

- If people disagree about blasphemy and naturalness, could they agree that biotechnology is disrespectful?
- What exactly is being disrespected?

## Is (modern) biotech disrespectful? Reductionist vs. Holistic Arguments

### The Reductionist Argument: (-Jeremy Rifkin)

- Reductionism looks at living organisms mainly in terms of genes, molecules and chemicals.
- Critics argue that transferring genes simply as chemical codes may remove the idea that species/life have something special or sacred.
- According to this argument:
  - Life becomes reduced to chemicals.
  - Life becomes less sacred.
  - Living organisms become increasingly available for manipulation.

## Is (modern) biotech disrespectful? Reductionist vs. Holistic Arguments

### The holistic, ecological or environmental arguments

- All forms of life are interconnected.
- Life exists as a complex, self-regulating **biotic community**.
- Therefore, moral value may extend beyond humans to the non-human world.
- Biotechnology may be criticised for failing to respect this interconnectedness.
- The World Council of Churches position presented in the material argues that biotechnology can reflect a worldview that fails to respect humanity's dependence on the earth as the source of life and nourishment.

## Biotech is disrespectful….the reductionist arguments

- Many areas of modern science use reductionist approaches.
- Reductionism does not mean scientists consider humans to be nothing more than chemicals.
- Scientists may study humans statistically or chemically without disrespecting them.
- Reductionism is not automatically immoral.
- Ethical concern may differ depending on what is genetically manipulated:
  - Plants
  - Animals
- Genetic manipulation of a tomato may raise different concerns from manipulation of a dog.
- Respect for the environment may be a more important issue than reductionism itself.

## Biotech is disrespectful….the Holistic arguments

- Does biotechnology really show a lack of respect for nature?
- Specialist knowledge may actually increase respect and understanding.
- For example, physicians treating humans do not automatically become disrespectful of humans.
- Ethical distinctions may exist between:
  - Genetic manipulation of animals
  - Genetic manipulation of plants
- Traditional biotechnology often achieved similar goals.
- This raises the question of why modern biotechnology alone should be considered disrespectful.

## Crop Biotech- Extrinsic concerns

Extrinsic concerns focus on the **undesirable consequences** of crop biotechnology.

Main areas:

- Safety
- Socio-economic consequences

Important point:

- Safety and socio-economic consequences are predictions.
- Predictions may:
  - Come true
  - Not come true
- Therefore, extrinsic concerns are provisional.
- Their ethical importance depends partly on how likely the predicted consequences are.

## Drivers of Extrinsic Concerns

### 1. Difficulty in reaching agreements

- People may disagree about the consequences of an action.
- Even if everyone agrees about a consequence, that does not automatically prove that the consequence is morally right or wrong.

### 2. Multiplicity of Possible Consequences

- An action usually produces many consequences rather than only one.
- These consequences may occur at different times.
- Some may be positive while others may be negative.
- Therefore, advantages and disadvantages can conflict.

### 3. Costs and benefits of Consequences

- Different costs and benefits must be compared.
- Financial calculations alone cannot settle moral questions.
- Ethical judgement is required to decide:
  - Which benefits are more important?
  - Which harms are more serious?
  - Which should receive priority?
  - ## Extrinsic Concern…Is crop Biotech Risky? (Safety)

Two contrasting positions:

1. Large-scale introduction of genetically engineered bacteria, viruses, plants and animals could create unsafe outcomes.
2. Billions of organisms have been used/tested without producing the feared pathogens, while traditional breeding has also created new varieties without creating the same safety concerns.

## Is Crop biotech Risky?.....

- Risk does not automatically make an action morally wrong.
- Risk becomes an ethical issue when it raises questions about:
  - Responsibility
  - Safety
  - Justification
- Possible fears include:
  - Spread of super weeds
  - Loss of genetic diversity
  - Other unknown risks

## …..Is Crop biotech Risky?

### Some guidance positions

- Many possible harmful effects of crop biotechnology are still speculative.
- No actual examples of some predicted harms have occurred in practice.
- Some scientists believe the clean safety record suggests modern biotechnology is relatively safe.
- They argue that it may even be safer than some uncontrolled genetic exchanges occurring through traditional breeding.
- Countries where biotechnology is developed generally have strict regulations.
- Excessive caution may not eliminate risk.
- Too much caution could also prevent development of technologies needed to handle future challenges.

## Extrinsic Concern…Is crop biotech unfair?

Two contrasting positions:

1. Technological progress may put farmers on a continuous "treadmill" where they must keep adopting new technologies.
2. Every technological revolution creates social changes and problems that governments and societies must manage.

Important points:

- Technology itself cannot solve every social problem.
- The existence of problems is not automatically a reason to stop technological progress.
- The challenge is to create appropriate regulations that are:
  - Open
  - Transparent
  - Inclusive

## Is crop Biotech unfair?....

Main concerns are about poorer farmers and poorer countries.

Possible disadvantages include:

- Large-scale agriculture favouring powerful producers.
- Farmers being unable to reuse seeds.
- Restrictions on exchange of genetic resources.
- Patents and corporate control.
- Loss of niche products/capabilities.
- Loss of independence.
- Misappropriation of genetic resources from developing countries.

## ….Is crop Biotech unfair?

### Some Guidance positions

- New technologies usually create large socio-economic changes.
- This is not unique to crop biotechnology.
- New technologies initially tend to benefit:
  - Countries that develop them
  - People who have the ability to use them
- Patents can encourage inventions and innovation by giving inventors economic benefits.
- International actions can help prevent or reduce socio-economic burdens on poor and vulnerable communities.

## Animal Ethics vs. Animal Welfare

### Why do animals matter ethically?

- The main ethical concern is increasingly focused on **animal welfare**, rather than simply whether animals are used.
- Animals matter ethically because many animals are **sentient**.

### Sentiency

- **Sentiency = ability to experience pain and pleasure.**

### Speciesism

- **Speciesism = refusing to give all sentient beings equal moral consideration.**
- It usually means giving humans greater moral preference than other animals.

Two approaches to sentiency:

### Utilitarianism

- Maximise pleasure.
- Minimise pain.
- Compare human benefits with animal suffering.
- Main question:
  - Is the human benefit large enough to justify the animal suffering?

### Inherent Value

- Animals have value as individuals.
- Their value does not depend only on calculating pain and pleasure.
- Because they have inherent value, they deserve respectful treatment.
- A difficulty is that animals differ greatly across species.

## Animal Biotech- Intrinsic Concerns

Animal biotechnology may be considered intrinsically wrong if it is:

- Blasphemous
- Unnatural
- Disrespectful
- Problematic because of patenting

The arguments are similar to plant biotechnology, but animal biotechnology has additional concerns because animals are sentient.

Concerns can also differ depending on whether animal biotechnology is used for:

- Medicine
- Food

## Some questions peculiar to animal biotech

Transgenic animals may create special religious concerns such as:

- Halal
- Unclean
- Forbidden

Questions include:

- Would you eat a genetically modified turkey?
- What ethical concerns would you have?
- Should genes from a non-halal animal be used to improve a halal animal?
- What other religious/ethical concerns might arise?

## Animal Biotech- Extrinsic Concerns

Extrinsic concerns focus on undesirable consequences of animal biotechnology.

Main areas:

- Safety/risk
- Socio-economic consequences

Important:

- These consequences are predictions.
- They may or may not happen.
- Therefore, their ethical importance depends on how likely and serious they are.

Animal biotech extrinsic concerns are broadly similar to those of crop biotechnology.

## Risk concerns in Animal Biotech

Possible risks include:

- Biotechnology can change animals much faster than traditional breeding.
- This makes it difficult to observe effects across many generations.
- Unexpected harmful characteristics could appear in animals used as food.
- Genetic diversity may decrease.
- Monocultures could become vulnerable to:
  - New diseases
  - Environmental threats
- Genetically engineered research animals could escape and infect humans or other animals.
- They could potentially produce new or more resistant disease strains.
- Organs from GM animals used in human medicine could potentially transmit viral diseases.
- GM animals could accidentally or deliberately be released into the environment.
- Such releases could potentially cause ecological damage.
- Even unlikely risks should not be ignored.
- Regulatory bodies exist to ensure these risks are properly considered.

## Environmental Concerns in Biotech

Main concern:

- GMOs are novel and may unintentionally:
  - Reduce biodiversity
  - Change biodiversity
  - Disturb the balance of nature

Possible concerns:

- Transgene escape to wild-type organisms.
- Horizontal gene transfer.
- Creation of new disease agents.
- GM plants gaining a selective advantage.
- Development of **super-weeds**.
- Mixing genes from unrelated species.
- Crossing of species boundaries.
- Development of pesticide tolerance.
- Increased pesticide use.
- Harm to dependent wildlife.
- Resistance in insect pests.
- Harm to non-target organisms.
- Loss of crop and wildlife biodiversity.
- Loss of genetic diversity within crops.
- Unpredictable gene expression and gene flow ("genetic pollution").
- Changes in evolutionary patterns.
- Loss of ecosystems in marginal lands.
- Conversion of marginal lands to agriculture.
- Agricultural intensification.
- Contamination of soil and water.

## Environmental Ethics in Biotech

Environmental ethics is generally divided into:

- Human Centred Environmental ethics
- Eco centred Environmental ethics

### Human centred Environmental ethics

- Environment is valued mainly because humans depend on it.
- It should be protected so that its resources remain available for:
  - Current generations
  - Future generations

### Eco centred Environmental ethics

- Environment has value **in itself**, not only because it benefits humans.
- Nature may have intrinsic value because:
  - It is naturally created.
  - It forms part of the natural order.
  - God created it.
- Humans are considered custodians/stewards rather than absolute owners.

Both approaches agree:

- Humans are part of the biosphere.
- Humans must protect the environment to survive sustainably.

## Human Centred Environmental Ethics

- Humans depend on the environment for life and quality of life.
- Quality of life also depends on biodiversity.
- Humans may enjoy living alongside natural organisms or simply knowing that they exist.
- Ecosystems have delicate balances.
- Introducing new organisms may disturb this balance.
- The environment should be protected for both:
  - Present generations
  - Future generations

## Eco-centred Environmental Ethics

- These arguments are more intrinsic than human-centred arguments.
- They are often influenced by religious beliefs.

Main ideas:

- Nature has value simply because it exists.
- Other species should not be damaged unless absolutely necessary for human survival.
- Damage should not be justified merely for luxury.
- Living nature has value.
- Nature created by God has value.
- Humans are stewards, not owners, of the planet.
- Some argue that organisms have rights.
- Biotechnology may raise questions about whether interference with the biosphere is unnatural.

## Potential benefits to the Environment

Biotechnology/GM crops may potentially:

- Increase crop yield.
- Reduce water use.
- Reduce pesticide use.
- Reduce herbicide use.
- Improve weed control.
- Allow direct sowing without ploughing.
- Reduce moisture loss.
- Slightly increase the growing season.
- Control erosion in erosion-prone soils.
- Provide possible climate benefits.
- Help reclaim marginal land for wildlife because higher crop yields may reduce pressure to use additional land.

## Decision

- Environmental effects of biotechnology are complex.
- Ethical considerations are also complex.
- Decisions should be based on balancing:
  - Benefits
  - Risks

## Elements of Strong and Elements of Weak Justification (1h).

### Unit 3: Lecture 4; Elements of Strong and Elements of Weak Justification (1h).

The aim is to understand that good bioethical justification requires:

- Asking the correct key questions.
- Identifying core ethical considerations.
- Using an appropriate ethical framework.
- Giving logical reasons for the final decision.

## ……Recap on Ethics and Bioethics

### Ethics

- Ethics deals with deciding what a person should do.
- It applies to individuals as well as members of a community.
- Ethics also involves giving reasons to support decisions.

### Bioethics

- Bioethics is a branch of ethics dealing with ethical questions related to life sciences.
- Bioethical analysis helps:
  - Individuals
  - Governments
  - Organisations
  - Communities
  make decisions about biological knowledge and innovations.

## Why teach bioethics & bioethical analysis?

### Advance students’ science understanding

- Bioethics connects science with society.
- Real-world ethical situations can make science more meaningful.

### Prepare students to make informed, thoughtful choices

- Bioethics improves understanding of biotechnology and its effects on society.
- Helps people make better-informed decisions.

### Promote respectful dialogue among people with diverse views

- Bioethics encourages discussion between people with different:
  - Backgrounds
  - Beliefs
  - Values
- It teaches people to consider different viewpoints respectfully.

### Cultivate critical-reasoning skills

- Bioethics teaches students to give reasons for their opinions.
- It emphasises justification and logical thinking.

## Basic concepts in Bioethical analysis

Ethical analysis should consider four key questions:

1. What is the ethical question?
2. What are the relevant facts?
3. Who or what could be affected by the way the question gets resolved?
4. What are the relevant ethical considerations?

Important:

- A good decision should consider all relevant factors.
- The final decision does not necessarily have to be universally accepted.
- Different viewpoints should be recognised.
- The main goal is to provide a proper **justification**.
- The questions do not have to be answered in a fixed order.

## Guide to considering the key questions

Three major considerations should guide decisions:

### Respect for persons

- Respect people's rights, values and choices.

### Minimizing harms while maximizing benefits

- Try to reduce harm and increase benefits.
- Prefer actions that create the most benefit with the least harm.

### Fairness

- Benefits, risks and burdens should be distributed fairly.

These principles form an important basis for:

- Utilitarianism
- Deontology
- Virtue ethics
- Ethical decision-making

## What is the ethical question?

Identifying the ethical question requires:

### Moral imagination / moral sensitivity

- Ability to notice the ethical dimension of a situation.
- It means recognising that an ethical issue exists.

### Distinguishing an ethical question from other questions

Ethical questions should be distinguished from:

- Legal questions
- Scientific questions
- Personal preferences
- Religious questions

These may be related but they are not the same type of question.

## What are the relevant facts?

- Ethical analysis requires relevant facts.
- Scientific facts connect science with ethical decisions.
- They help determine possible:
  - Benefits
  - Harms

Social science information may also be relevant, including:

- Psychological facts
- Sociological facts
- Anthropological facts
- Historical facts
- Economic facts
- Religious facts/concepts

Important:

- Sometimes decisions must be made with incomplete information.
- Research should be used to gather as much information as possible.
- Decisions should remain open to change if new facts become available.

Potential stakeholders include:

- Individuals
- Groups
- Institutions

- Identifies who/what may be affected.

### Respect for persons

- Do not treat people merely as a means to achieve a goal.
- Do not unnecessarily interfere with people's choices.
- Help people understand their options and make their own decisions.

### Minimizing harms while maximizing benefits

- Balance harm and benefit.
- Prefer the action that:
  - Produces more benefit
  - Produces less harm

### Fairness

- Fairness includes:
  - Justice
  - Distributive justice
  - Social justice

### others

Other considerations may include:

- Authenticity

## Building and assessing Justifications

A good ethical decision requires:

1. Asking the ethical questions.
2. Collecting all relevant facts.
3. Identifying all possible stakeholders.
4. Considering all options using relevant ethical principles.

A recommendation must explain:

- Why is this decision the best?
- Which ethical considerations are important?
- How does the recommended action address them?
- What alternatives were considered?
- Why were those alternatives rejected?

This explanation is called **JUSTIFICATION**.

## Building Strong Justifications

Elements of a strong justification:

- Highly relevant to the ethical question.
- Uses the most important scientific and social-science facts.
- Explains how the decision affects other people/stakeholders.
- Identifies and correctly applies relevant ethical considerations.
- Explains how the recommended action satisfies those considerations.
- Discusses strengths and weaknesses of alternative solutions.
- Uses logical reasoning.
- The conclusion must logically follow from the reasons.

The strongest justification:

- Gives strong reasons for the conclusion.
- Responds to counterarguments.
- Recognises that ethical questions may not always have one universally correct answer.
- Treats disagreements as opportunities for deeper thinking.

- Incorrect facts about the situation or its history.

- Incorrect understanding/application of ethical principles.
- Logical errors.
- A conclusion that does not follow from the reasons provided.

# BIOTECHNOLOGY
# PRINCIPLES AND PROCESSES

- The term Biotechnology was coined by Karl Ereky in 1919.
- The word combines Biology + Technology.
- Biotechnology uses living organisms, cells, enzymes or biological systems to produce useful products/processes.

## Principles of Biotechnology

### Genetic engineering

- Genetic engineering changes DNA/RNA in a laboratory to produce a desired characteristic or product.
- It can introduce selected genes into host organisms.
- Sterile conditions are maintained when growing desired microorganisms/cells for products such as:
  - Antibiotics
  - Vaccines
  - Enzymes

## The techniques of genetic engineering

- Creation of recombinant DNA.
- Gene cloning.
- Gene transfer.
- Allows desirable genes to be selected without carrying unwanted genes along with them.
- Overcomes some limitations of traditional hybridisation.

## Basic steps involved in process

General sequence:

1. Isolate DNA from donor.
2. Cut DNA into fragments using restriction enzymes.
3. Identify the desired gene.
4. Insert desired gene into a cloning vector.
5. Introduce recombinant DNA into a host cell.
6. Multiply the DNA/host cells.
7. Obtain the desired product.

## “Genetic Engineering”

- Production of new combinations of genetic material artificially in the laboratory.

## Insulin production by Genetic Engineering

- Human insulin gene can be inserted into microorganisms such as E. coli.
- The microorganisms produce insulin.
- The insulin is then collected and purified for medical use.

## Basic steps in rDNA

1. Identify DNA containing desirable genes.
2. Introduce the identified DNA into the host.
3. Maintain the introduced DNA in the host and allow it to be transferred to progeny/copies.

## Tools of Recombinant DNA Technology

### Restriction Endo Nucleases(REN)

- Act as "molecular scissors."
- Cut DNA at specific recognition sequences.

### DNA Ligase

- Joins DNA molecules/fragments together.

### Cloning Vectors (Vehicles for Cloning)

- Carry foreign DNA into a host cell.
- Examples:
  - Plasmids
  - Cosmids
  - Bacteriophages

### Host cell

- Cell into which recombinant DNA is introduced.
- Common example: E. coli.

### Bioreactors

- Special containers where biological reactions occur under controlled conditions.

## Restriction Endonucleases

- Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific positions.
- They recognise specific DNA sequences called recognition sequences.

## Example

### Hind II

- Recognises a particular six-base DNA sequence.
- Cuts DNA at a specific point.

## EcoRI

- Recognises the sequence GAATTC.
- Cuts between specific bases.
- Can produce **sticky ends**.
- The same enzyme can cut vector DNA and foreign DNA so their ends can pair.

## ‘Sticky End’ Restriction Endonucleases

- Produce DNA ends with complementary overhangs.
- These overhangs easily pair with matching DNA ends.

## ‘Blunt End’ Restriction Endonucleases

- Produce straight DNA ends.
- No overhang is produced.

## Recombinant DNA formation

- Same restriction enzyme cuts:
  - Foreign DNA
  - Vector DNA
- Compatible ends are produced.
- DNA ligase joins the foreign DNA to the vector.

## Convention for naming REN

Naming is based on the organism from which the enzyme was isolated:

- First letter = genus.
- Next two letters = species.
- Fourth letter = strain.
- Roman numeral = order in which the enzyme was discovered.

Example:
- EcoRI
- HindIII

## Nomenclature of REN

### EcoRI
- E = genus
- co = species
- I = first enzyme discovered

### HindIII
- H = Haemophilus
- in = influenzae
- d = strain Rd
- III = third endonuclease discovered

## Gel Electrophoresis

- Restriction enzymes create DNA fragments.
- Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments according to size.
- DNA is negatively charged and moves towards the positive electrode.

## Separated DNA fragments are visualized

- DNA fragments can be stained and viewed under UV light.
- Bands appear on the gel.
- Desired DNA bands can be cut from the agarose gel.
- DNA is then extracted from the gel.
- This extraction step is called **Elution**.
- Purified DNA can then be used to construct recombinant DNA.

## Cloning Vectors (Vehicles for Cloning):

### Plasmids

- Small, circular, double-stranded DNA molecules.
- Usually exist outside the bacterial chromosome.
- Can replicate independently.
- Term "plasmid" was introduced by J. Lederberg in 1952.

A good vector should have:
- Origin of replication (ori).
- Selectable marker.
- At least one unique restriction-enzyme site.
- Relatively small size.

Common vectors:
- Plasmids
- Bacteriophages
- Ti-plasmids
- Disarmed retroviruses

## Plasmid pUC 19 Cloning Vector

Important features:
- Ampicillin resistance gene.
- lacZ' gene.
- Used for identifying recombinant DNA.
- "UC" refers to University of California.

## Plasmid pBR 322

- Size: 4,361 bp.
- Contains:
  - Ampicillin resistance
  - Tetracycline resistance
  - Replication region
- pBR refers to Bolivar and Rodriguez.

## Identification of recombinants: Insertional inactivation

- A foreign DNA fragment is inserted into a gene in the vector.
- This disrupts the normal function of that gene.
- This disruption can be used to identify recombinant plasmids.

## Blue-White selection.

- Based on insertional inactivation of the **lacZ** gene.
- lacZ normally produces beta-galactosidase.
- Functional lacZ → blue colonies.
- Foreign DNA inserted into lacZ → gene becomes inactive → white colonies.

Therefore:
- **White = recombinant**
- **Blue = non-recombinant**

## Competent Host (Introduction of recombinant DNA into host cells):

### Transformation

- Host cells take up DNA from their surroundings.
- DNA normally cannot easily cross the cell membrane.
- Calcium ions can make cells more capable of taking up plasmid DNA.

### Heat Shock

- DNA and cells are kept on ice.
- Briefly heated to about 42°C.
- Returned to ice.
- Helps bacteria take up recombinant DNA.

### Microinjection

- DNA is directly injected into the nucleus using a very fine glass micropipette.

### Biolistics / Gene gun method

- Mainly used for plant cells.
- Tiny gold/tungsten particles are coated with DNA.
- Particles are shot into cells.

### “Disarmed Pathogen” Vectors

- Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers T-DNA into plant cells.
- Its Ti plasmid can be modified so it is no longer pathogenic.
- The modified vector can deliver desired genes into plants.

## Transgenic plants with Ti plasmid

- Modified Ti plasmid transfers desired DNA into plant cells.
- These cells can develop into transgenic plants.

## Processes of Recombinant DNA Technology

1. Isolation of DNA.
2. Fragmentation using restriction endonucleases.
3. Isolation of desired DNA fragment.
4. Ligation into a vector.
5. Transfer of recombinant DNA into host.
6. Culturing host cells.
7. Extraction and purification of desired product.

## Isolation of DNA

- DNA should be isolated in pure form.
- Cell walls can be broken using enzymes:
  - Lysozyme → bacteria
  - Cellulase → plant cells
  - Chitinase → fungi
- RNA can be removed using ribonuclease.
- Histone/protein contamination can be removed using proteases.

## Fragmentation of DNA

- Purified DNA is treated with restriction enzymes.
- DNA fragments are separated using appropriate methods.
- Desired DNA and vector DNA are cut using the same restriction enzyme.
- Desired fragment and vector are mixed with ligase.
- Recombinant DNA is produced.

## PCR

### Polymerase Chain Reaction

- PCR rapidly makes many copies of a specific DNA region.
- Uses:
  - Template DNA
  - Primers
  - DNA polymerase
  - Taq polymerase
  - Deoxynucleotides
- Repeated cycles can produce millions/billions of copies.
- Amplified DNA can then be used for further cloning or analysis.

## Insertion of Recombinant DNA into the Host Cell / Organism

- Recombinant DNA is introduced into host cells.
- Example:
  - Plasmid containing ampicillin-resistance gene enters E. coli.
  - Transformed bacteria become ampicillin-resistant.
  - Growing them on ampicillin kills non-transformed cells.
- The antibiotic-resistance gene acts as a **selectable marker**.

## Recombinant protein

- Host cells can be grown in the laboratory.
- Desired protein can be extracted and purified.
- A protein produced from a gene expressed in a different/heterologous host is a recombinant protein.

## Bioreactors

- Large vessels where biological materials are converted into useful products.
- Can use:
  - Microbial cells
  - Plant cells
  - Animal cells
  - Human cells
- Provide controlled:
  - Temperature
  - pH
  - Nutrients/substrate
  - Salts
  - Vitamins
  - Oxygen

## Simple Stirred-tank bioreactor

Main features:
- Cylindrical/curved vessel.
- Stirrer keeps contents mixed.
- Air can be bubbled through the reactor.
- Agitator system.
- Oxygen delivery system.
- Foam control.
- Temperature control.
- pH control.
- Sampling ports.
- Sterilisation system.

## Spranged stirred tank Bioreactor

- Designed to increase oxygen-transfer efficiency.
- Bubbles increase the surface area through which oxygen enters the culture.

## Downstream Processing

- Separation and purification of the desired product.
- Includes recovery of product from the biological mixture.
- Strict quality-control testing is required.

## What are the Ethical Concerns Associated with Biotechnology?

## Stem Cell Research

- Studies stem cells to understand disease and develop treatments.
- Potential applications include blood disorders, cancers and other diseases.

### Ethical issues Associated: Destruction of Embryos

- Embryonic stem-cell research may involve destroying human embryos.
- Supporters argue that this may produce major medical benefits.
- Opponents may consider embryo destruction morally similar to taking a human life.

### Informed Consent and Exploitation

- Stem cells may come from embryos left over from IVF.
- Donors must properly understand how embryos will be used.
- Concerns exist about exploitation of vulnerable donors.
- The Belmont Report stresses:
  - Respect for persons
  - Beneficence
  - Justice

### Equity and Access

- Advanced stem-cell treatments may be expensive.
- Wealthier people may receive greater access.
- This can increase existing healthcare inequalities.
- Fair distribution of biotech benefits is therefore important.

## Cloning

### About

- Cloning creates genetically identical copies by reproducing an organism's genetic blueprint.
- Clones are produced asexually and are genetically very similar/identical to the source.

### Types

#### Reproductive Cloning

- Creates a genetically identical organism.
- Somatic-cell nucleus is transferred into an enucleated egg.
- Embryo is implanted into a surrogate.
- Example: Dolly the sheep.

#### Therapeutic Cloning

- Creates a cloned embryo to obtain embryonic stem cells.
- Intended for research or treatment of diseases such as:
  - Parkinson's
  - Alzheimer's
  - Diabetes
- May allow patient-specific stem-cell lines.

#### Gene Cloning (Molecular Cloning)

- Produces many copies of a specific gene/DNA segment.
- Desired DNA is inserted into a vector such as a plasmid.
- Host cells such as bacteria multiply the DNA.
- Example: cloning the human insulin gene in E. coli.

#### Embryo Cloning (Artificial Twinning)

- Early embryo is split into two or more genetically identical embryos.
- Similar to natural identical twins.
- Used particularly in agriculture/livestock breeding.

### Associated Ethical Issues:

#### Identity and Individuality

- Raises questions about whether a genetically identical person would have the same identity or individuality.
- Human identity also depends on experiences and development, not only genes.

#### Health Risks

- Cloning has historically had low success rates.
- Cloned animals may suffer health problems.
- Surrogate mothers can also face risks.
- Dolly experienced health problems and had a relatively short life.

#### Exploitation of Animals

- Surrogate animals may undergo repeated invasive procedures.
- Cloning can result in:
  - Failed pregnancies
  - Miscarriages
  - Stillbirths
  - Animal suffering
- Dolly's cloning involved many attempts, showing the high biological cost involved.

## Genetic Engineering

### About

- Manipulation of an organism's genetic material by adding, removing or changing genes.
- Used in:
  - Agriculture
  - Medicine
  - Environmental conservation

## Genetic Engineering Technologies:

### CRISPR/Cas9 Technology

- CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing technology used to make precise DNA changes.

Main components:
- **Guide RNA:** Directs Cas9 to the target DNA.
- **Cas9:** Acts like molecular scissors and cuts the DNA.

### Applications

#### Agriculture

- Increased crop yield.
- Example: rice edited for resistance to bacterial blight.

#### Medicine
- Potential treatment of genetic disorders.
- Used in research involving diseases such as sickle-cell anaemia and cystic fibrosis.

#### Conservation
- Potential use in endangered species conservation and attempts to recreate traits of extinct species.

### Ethical Concerns

- Off-target mutations.
- Germline editing.
- Designer babies.
- Biodiversity/ecological effects.
- Long-term effects of GMOs.
- Unequal access to genetic technologies.
- Genetic discrimination in:
  - Employment
  - Insurance
  - Social services

# FOUNDATION OF BIOETHICS

## Definition

### Ethics

- Ethics is the branch of philosophy dealing with the moral aspects of human behaviour.

### Health ethics

- Health ethics is applied ethics concerned with moral values and decisions in healthcare.

## Medical ethics

- Medical ethics is a set of guidelines concerning the ideal relationship between physicians, patients and colleagues.
- The Oath of Hippocrates is a classic example.

## Medical ethics

- Physicians have a moral, and sometimes legal, duty to act in the patient's best interest.
- They should use the best available information.
- A major question is: What exactly is "good" for the patient, and who decides it?

## A number of reasons are responsible for the interest in health ethics

- Major scientific and technological advances have created new ethical problems.
- Old ethical problems have also become more complicated.
- Medicine has moved from **paternalism** towards **autonomy**.
- More healthcare professionals are involved:
  - Specialists
  - Nurses/health professionals
  - Students
  - Administrators
  - Researchers
- Society is increasingly involved through:
  - Media
  - Courts
  - Legislators

## Ethics and the Law

### Ethical decisions
- Aim at what is best/common good.
- May require more than what the law requires.

### Legal decisions
- Follow laws, statutes and case law.

## Important principles

- Scientific and humanistic aspects must both be considered.
- Patient feelings and values matter.
- These values may be:
  - Personal
  - Social
  - Cultural
  - Economic

## Difference between research and practice

### Practice
- Treatment/diagnosis intended to benefit a particular patient.
- Has a reasonable expectation of success.

### Research
- Designed to test a hypothesis and produce generalizable knowledge.

## Institution Review Bodies (IRBs)

An IRB should:

- Identify research risks.
- Minimize risks.
- Identify expected benefits.
- Compare risks with benefits.
- Ensure participants receive accurate information about:
  - Risks
  - Discomforts
  - Benefits
- Ensure voluntary participation.
- Allow participants to refuse or withdraw without coercion or punishment.
- Conduct periodic review.
- Ensure appropriate monitoring of data collection.

## Ethical Principles

1. **Beneficence** – act in the patient's best interest.
2. **Non-malfeasance** – "first, do no harm."
3. **Autonomy** – patient can choose or refuse treatment.
4. **Justice** – fair distribution of limited healthcare resources.
5. **Non discriminatory treatment** – people should not be unfairly discriminated against.
6. **Dignity** – patients and healthcare providers deserve dignity.
7. **Truthfulness and honesty** – truthful communication and informed consent are important.

Historical events such as:
- Doctors' Trial of the Nuremberg trials
- Tuskegee Syphilis Study

increased the importance of informed consent and honesty.

## Conflicts between values

- Ethical principles can conflict.
- Example:
  - Patient autonomy says respect the patient's refusal.
  - Beneficence says provide life-saving treatment.
- Therefore, ethical decision-making often requires balancing principles.

## Beneficence

- Moral duty to actively do good for others.
- The duty has limits.
- The amount of help expected depends on:
  - Degree of need
  - Ability to help
  - Relationship between people

## Autonomy

### Paternalism

- Physician chooses treatment because their knowledge and experience are considered best for the patient.

### Autonomy

- Patient has the right to make decisions about their own treatment.
- Patient must receive enough information to make an informed decision.
- Physician's personal values should not replace the patient's final decision.

### Drawbacks of autonomy

- Patient may not fully understand the medical situation.
- Poor decisions may cause avoidable harm.

## Autonomy

- Respect for autonomy supports:
  - Informed consent
  - Advance directives
- A person's decision-making capacity may need to be assessed when autonomy and beneficence conflict.

## Non-malfeasance

- Obligation not to harm others.
- Includes preventing foreseeable harm.
- "DO NO HARM!!"

## Double effect

- An action may produce both:
  - A desired positive effect
  - A harmful side effect
- Example:
  - Morphine can reduce severe pain but may also suppress breathing and potentially hasten death.

## Principles checklist/aide memoire

### Non-Maleficence

Ask:
- Will anyone be harmed?
- Are children protected from harm?

### Beneficence

Ask:
- Does the intervention benefit participants?
- Overall, are benefits greater than harms?

## Health Maximization

Ask:
- Is the intervention effective and evidence-based?
- Does it improve population health?
- Is the effect sustainable long-term?
- Does the community gain additional value?

## Efficiency

Ask:
- Is the intervention cost-effective?

## Respect for Autonomy

Ask:
- Is coercion/manipulation avoided?
- Is free choice encouraged?
- Is there genuine informed consent?
- Can people actually exercise self-responsibility?
- Are privacy and personal data protected?
- If paternalism is used, is it justified?
- Does the intervention promote autonomy?

## Justice

Ask:
- Is anyone stigmatized, discriminated against or excluded?
- Is the institution acting transparently?
- Are vulnerable groups protected?
- Does the intervention increase social/health inequalities?
- Are vulnerable groups such as migrants supported?
- Does it preserve social cohesion and solidarity?

## Proportionality

Ask:
- Is this the least restrictive option?
- Are costs proportional to benefits/utility?

## Steps of applied ethical reasoning;

1. Identify the underlying moral conflict.
2. Identify relevant ethical principles.
3. Ask whose autonomy is affected.
4. Collect all relevant information.
5. Identify possible benefits and harms.
6. Consider alternative solutions.
7. Compare the alternatives ethically.
8. Weigh the conflicting principles.
9. Decide on the best solution.
10. Check whether you personally and professionally can accept the conclusion.

## Case study-2

A 20-year-old student has severe bacterial meningitis.
- High fever.
- Neck rigidity.
- Cloudy spinal fluid.
- Bacterial infection diagnosed.
- Antibiotics are necessary.

# implications of ethics in practice

## Informed Consent

- Without proper information, a patient may make a decision that does not reflect their real values.
- Informed consent is closely connected with:
  - Autonomy
  - Truth-telling

## Other principles

### Patient Rights and Responsibilities.

- Patients have rights and responsibilities regarding their care.

### Advance Directives

- Patients can specify future treatment preferences.
- They can delegate decision-making authority to another person.
- If incapacitated, an appropriate representative/next-of-kin may make decisions.

## Do Not Resuscitate (DNR).

- Usually governed by relevant state/local rules.
- Requires physician order.
- Must be documented.
- Requires clear policies/procedures.
- Patient, family and staff should receive appropriate education.
- Does not necessarily require an advance directive.

## Confidentiality

Confidentiality can become difficult when:
- A patient has a sexually transmitted disease but refuses to inform a spouse.
- An underage patient terminates a pregnancy without parents' knowledge.

The challenge is balancing privacy against possible harm to others.

## Importance of Communication

- Many ethical conflicts come from poor communication.
- Communication problems can occur between:
  - Patient and healthcare team
  - Family members
  - Healthcare professionals
- Better communication can prevent disagreements.

## Ethics committee

- Used when communication alone cannot resolve a difficult ethical conflict.
- Hospitals may establish permanent ethics committees as ethical issues increase.
- Members can include:
  - Healthcare professionals
  - Religious leaders
  - Community representatives

## Truth- telling

- Different cultures have different expectations about telling patients their diagnosis.
- Some cultures place less emphasis on directly informing patients, especially about serious diseases such as cancer.

## Conflict of interest

- Physicians should not allow personal interests to affect medical judgement.
- Some conflicts are difficult to avoid.
- Doctors should recognise and manage them.

## Referral

- Financial incentives can influence referrals.
- Example: doctors receiving income from medical-test referrals may refer more patients for those tests.

## Vendor relationship

- Pharmaceutical companies may influence doctors through:
  - Gifts
  - Food
  - Sponsored educational programmes
- Industry-sponsored CME can influence prescribing.
- Efforts exist to reduce pharmaceutical marketing influence.

## Withholding and Withdrawing Treatment

### Life-sustaining treatment

Treatment intended to prolong life without reversing the underlying medical condition.

Examples:
- Mechanical ventilation
- Renal dialysis
- Artificial nutrition and hydration
- Antibiotics
- Blood products

Requires clear policies and procedures.

## Capacity

A patient should be able to:
- Understand the situation.
- Understand consequences of the decision.
- Communicate their wishes.

Capacity is normally assessed by the physician.

## Futile Care

- Do not abandon the patient.
- Arrange transfer if necessary.
- Have appropriate policies.
- Decisions should be based on medical issues, not:
  - Age
  - Social status
  - Other irrelevant factors
- Avoid court involvement where possible.
- Use the Ethics Committee.

## Ethics committee

Should include:
- A well-trained chairperson.
- Appropriate medical/clinical staff.
- Religious/community representation.
- A layperson from the community.

## Ethics Committee

### Risk manager

Should:
- Remain neutral.
- Facilitate discussion.
- Provide legal consultation.
- Develop an ethics-consultation system.

## Topics for Ethics Committees

- Abortion and reproductive rights
- End-of-life/futile care
- Quality of life
- Advance directives
- Staff rights conflicting with patient wishes

# Key Ethical Theories in Bioethics

## Deontological Ethics (Duty-based)

- Key thinker: Immanuel Kant.
- Actions are morally right when they follow universal duties and respect persons.
- **Categorical Imperative:** Act only according to rules that could be made universal laws.
- Applications:
  - Prohibition of certain practices such as human cloning.
  - Respect autonomy.
  - Informed consent.
  - Never treat people merely as tools.

## Utilitarian Ethics (Consequentialist)

- Key thinkers:
  - Jeremy Bentham
  - John Stuart Mill
- Correct action = greatest good for the greatest number.
- Uses cost-benefit reasoning.
- Applications:
  - Research with low risk and high social benefit.
  - Healthcare resource allocation.
  - Risk-benefit analysis.

## Virtue Ethics

- Associated with Aristotle.
- Focuses on character rather than only rules or consequences.
- Important virtues:
  - Compassion
  - Integrity
  - Honesty
  - Courage
- In biotechnology, focuses on the moral character and integrity of scientists and healthcare professionals.

## Legal Impacts of Biotechnology

## Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

### Patent Definition

- A patent gives an inventor exclusive rights over an invention for a limited period, typically 20 years.
- It prevents others from making, using or selling the invention without permission.

### TRIPS Requirements

A patent generally requires:
- **Novelty** — must be new.
- **Non-obviousness** — must involve an inventive step.
- **Utility** — must have practical/industrial use.

### Patentable Subject Matter

Examples:
- Isolated/purified DNA
- GMOs
- Recombinant proteins
- Monoclonal antibodies
- Vaccines
- Diagnostic methods
- CRISPR technologies

### Non-Patentable Subject Matter

Examples:
- Naturally occurring substances in their natural state.
- Human embryos.
- Human cloning methods.
- Human germline modifications.

## Landmark Biotech Patent Cases

### Diamond v. Chakrabarty (US) — 1980
- Human-modified living organisms can be patentable.

### Moore v. Regents of Univ. of California (US) — 1990
- Patients do not automatically have property rights over cells removed from their body.
- Researchers have disclosure duties.

### Greenwood v. Greenwood (UK) — 1994
- Patent for erythropoietin (EPO) upheld.

### Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics (US) — 2013
- Naturally occurring DNA sequences are not patentable.
- cDNA can be patentable.

### Harvard Oncomouse (Canada) — 2002
- Higher life forms were not patentable in Canada.

### Brüstle v. Greenpeace (European Court) — 2011
- Patents involving destruction of human embryos were excluded from patentability.

### Natural vs. Artificial
- Biological products may be natural in substance but artificial in origin.
- This blurs the natural/artificial boundary.

### Life as Machine
- Treating organisms only as machines may change attitudes towards life.

### Creation of Life
- Creating living organisms from scratch raises questions about human responsibility.

### Living vs. Non-living
- Synthetic entities can challenge traditional definitions of life.

### Playing God
- Concern that humans may be crossing moral/ecological limits.

### Hubris
- Excessive confidence in humans' ability to control complex biological systems.

## Environmental Unpredictability & Precautionary Principle

### Unpredictability Types

- Horizontal gene transfer.
- Off-target gene-editing effects.
- Unexpected ecological interactions.
- Unpredictable evolutionary mutations.

### Precautionary Principle

When an activity may cause serious harm to health or the environment, precautionary measures should be taken even when science has not completely established the cause-and-effect relationship.
